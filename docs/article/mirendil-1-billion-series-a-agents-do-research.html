Mirendil Raised $1 Billion to Sell Agents That Do Research Work for Scientists

Mirendil, a San Francisco company founded by researchers who left Anthropic, xAI and OpenAI, announced a $1 billion Series A on Thursday, November 12, led by Kleiner Perkins and Clearlake Capital. The company says one researcher, directing AI agents, pretrained its Mirendil-Base coding model from scratch in three and a half months on less than $10 million of compute. That claim, and the model’s results, are the company’s own and have not been independently checked. Mirendil did not disclose a valuation.
Edited by Jordan Hale · Capital Editor  ·  Issue No. 026  ·  Friday, November 13, 2026

The largest cheque of the day went to a company that is five months out of the gate. Mirendil announced a $1 billion Series A on Thursday, led by Kleiner Perkins and Clearlake Capital, with Battery Ventures, Doerr Capital, Gil & Co, Google, M13, Pear VC and S32 also taking part. The company did not disclose a valuation. Bloomberg reported in September that Kleiner Perkins was in talks to lead a round of up to $1 billion at a $5 billion valuation; that figure is a report about the talks, not a disclosed price.

The company was founded in late 2025 by Behnam Neyshabur, now chief executive, and Harsh Mehta, now technology chief, both formerly of Anthropic, with co-founders from xAI and OpenAI. It launched in June with a team of twenty researchers and engineers drawn from Anthropic, xAI, Google DeepMind and OpenAI, and a $200 million seed round led by Andreessen Horowitz and Kleiner Perkins. Kleiner Perkins has now led or co-led both rounds.

The substance under the raise is a claim about how research labour divides. In an October 21 report, Mirendil said one researcher, directing AI agents, pretrained its Mirendil-Base coding model from scratch in three and a half months, at an all-in compute cost below $10 million, including experiments, data processing and the agents’ own token spending. An experienced researcher directed the work and judged the results; the model did not build itself. Mirendil then post-trained the base model into Fern, which it says holds up to ten million tokens of context. None of these figures has been independently evaluated.

The product is Lor, a cloud platform that went into a free, invitation-only private beta on October 28. Its agents write code, run experiments, bring up their own compute and work for days at a time, using models from several labs as well as Mirendil’s own. A multi-year Google Cloud partnership, announced in August, supplies the machines. What has to happen next is ordinary and hard: outside users have to reproduce results like the training run, and the private beta has to become a product companies pay for.
