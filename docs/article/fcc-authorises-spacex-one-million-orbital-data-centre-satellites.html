The FCC Authorised SpaceX to Deploy One Million Satellites That Would Work as Data Centres in Orbit

The Federal Communications Commission authorised SpaceX on Wednesday, November 25, to deploy up to one million satellites in low Earth orbit that would process data in space for use on Earth (Reuters). The constellation is called Starmind. The agency called it the first authorisation of its kind in the United States. The order does not require the first satellite to be operating until 2033, and the full constellation not until 2040. SpaceX sought the permission earlier this year, saying the satellites would help meet rapidly growing demand for AI computing.
Edited by Avery Lane · Frontier Editor  ·  Issue No. 039  ·  Thursday, November 26, 2026

The scale is the news and the limit at once. One million is roughly fifty times the number of active satellites in orbit today, and no part of the system has been built. SpaceX chief financial officer Bret Johnsen said last week the company plans to launch its first data centres into space in 2027 and expand through 2029 (the Wall Street Journal). Elon Musk has previously said the company could demonstrate the technology by late 2027, with broader deployment possibly beginning in 2028 (USA Today). Those company dates sit well ahead of the deadlines the FCC set: one authorised satellite operating by 2033, then a gradual build through 2040.

The stated appeal is power. A satellite in sunlight can run on solar energy without drawing on a grid, at a moment when data centres on Earth wait years for connections and, in the Colossus case this paper covered in Edition 014, for permits. The objections are physical and were filed with the regulator. Scientists quoted by the Associated Press question how chips in orbit would shed their heat — a data centre on Earth uses air and water for that, and a satellite has neither — and astronomers, along with Viasat and Amazon’s Kuiper Systems, asked the FCC to deny the application, citing orbital debris and interference with observation. The FCC said the plan is consistent with its orbital debris rules and attached conditions on communications backups, regular updates and a debris plan (GeekWire). FCC chairman Brendan Carr called the authorisation a historic first in the AI race.

What was decided on Wednesday is permission, on a fourteen-year clock, for a system whose central engineering problem is unsolved in public. SpaceX now holds the regulatory position before it holds the technology. The first test is the one the company set itself: a working orbital data centre, at a cost that can be compared with a building on the ground.

The Bigger Picture

Permission and capability are running on different clocks in this buildout, and Wednesday’s order separates them by years. SpaceX holds the first authorisation for orbital computing while the heat problem is still open and the first required satellite is due in 2033. The pattern matches the ground buildout, where the scarce asset has repeatedly been the signed approval — a grid connection, a permit, a licence — rather than the hardware. Whoever collects the approvals first owns the option, whichever technology arrives to fill it.
