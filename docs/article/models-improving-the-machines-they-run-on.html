The Models Have Started Improving the Machines They Run On

OpenAI’s models have rewritten circuits inside a chip AMD is sending to manufacturing. Anthropic’s prospectus describes a 15-person team designing custom chips with Claude. OpenAI’s newest model is rated High for speeding up AI research itself. Each claim is a company’s own. Together they describe a loop closing: the systems are becoming inputs to the production of their own next generation.
Edited by Avery Lane · Frontier Editor  ·  Issue No. 032  ·  Thursday, November 19, 2026

Start with what is documented, because the claims in this area inflate quickly. On Wednesday, OpenAI reported that its models rewrote circuit code for AMD’s flagship graphics processor, improved the design’s power, performance and area by up to 14% on particular blocks, and that AMD has put some of the changes into a design sent for manufacturing. On OpenAI’s own Jalapeño inference chip, model-proposed changes cut cell area by about 13% and 10% in two units and held up in finished silicon. These are OpenAI’s figures, published with OpenAI’s own caveats, and AMD has published none of its own. In Anthropic’s roadshow reporting, covered in Edition 028, the company described a team of 15 people designing custom chips with Claude’s help. And on Tuesday, OpenAI’s system card for GPT-6.2 Astra rated the model High for AI self-improvement — its ability to speed up AI research — a determination Anthropic’s prospectus made about its own programme in the same week (Editions 030 and 031).

None of this is a machine designing its successor alone. Chip design is the most checkable kind of engineering there is: a rewritten circuit either still computes the same function or it does not, and the tools that verify it are forty years old. That is exactly why it happened here first. A model can propose a thousand rewrites, and the verification machinery discards the wrong ones without a human reading them. The human role moves to choosing which blocks to attack and judging trade-offs the tools cannot price, such as whether a design that is faster but harder to manufacture is worth having. The 14% figure should be read with that structure in mind: it is the best block-level result from a very large number of machine-checked attempts, not a uniform improvement across a chip.

The economic consequence arrives before the philosophical one. Chip design has been a labour bottleneck with a fixed supply of experts; a verified year of optimisation in five months changes project schedules across the industry, and it changes them first for the labs’ own hardware programmes — Jalapeño, Anthropic’s custom silicon, Google’s TPUs — which exist to loosen Nvidia’s grip on compute supply. If the gains hold at production scale, the cost curve of compute bends a little further down, and it bends because the models did some of the bending. The checkpoint is concrete and near: whether AMD’s manufactured silicon, when it is measured in the open, carries the improvement the models were credited with. Until then, the loop is documented at the level of designs and filings, not yet at the level of shipped chips.
