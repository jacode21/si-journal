A New Kind of Home Test Can Detect Molecules Too Small for Today’s Test Strips

The strip tests in a bathroom cabinet — COVID, pregnancy — only work on large molecules with two places to grab. Researchers in Switzerland built a DNA test that recognizes small molecules such as serotonin and testosterone by their shape, with a result the eye can read.
Issue No. 025  ·  Thursday, November 12, 2026

The strip tests in a bathroom cabinet — COVID, pregnancy — only work on large molecules with two places to grab. Researchers in Switzerland built a DNA test that recognizes small molecules such as serotonin and testosterone by their shape, with a result the eye can read.

What it is

The strip test is a sandwich: it works when a molecule is large enough for two different binders to grab it at two places, and the line appears when both hold on. Small molecules — hormones, neurotransmitters, many drugs — offer only one place to grab, so the format has never worked for them. A team at EPFL in Switzerland used aptamers, short synthetic strands of DNA that fold around a target. One strand binds the small molecule and changes shape when it does. A second strand is engineered to recognize that changed shape, rather than the molecule itself — the sandwich closes on the binder, not the target. The team found working second strands for serotonin, testosterone and a steroid called DOG, and built a strip test that detected serotonin with a result visible to the naked eye.

Why it matters

A cheap, readable-at-home test for small molecules would move a class of measurements — hormone levels, some drug levels, neurotransmitter markers — out of laboratories and into clinics and homes that cannot run the machines those measurements currently need. The method is general in principle: any small molecule with a known aptamer is a candidate.

What is not yet known

This is a proof of concept: four molecules tried, three with working second strands, one strip test shown. No product exists, and sensitivity in real samples — blood or saliva rather than prepared solutions — has not been demonstrated.

Evidence — Peer-reviewed study

Peer-reviewed study: Stuber et al., Nature Chemistry (2026), reported by Phys.org, November 5, 2026.
