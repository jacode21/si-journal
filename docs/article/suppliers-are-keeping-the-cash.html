The Suppliers Are Keeping the Cash

Wednesday’s results drew a line through the buildout: the company selling the memory inside AI systems reported an 80% operating margin and a contracted order book, while the largest buyers split — one kept its cash flow positive, the other funded the quarter with outside money.
Edited by Avery Lane · Frontier Editor  ·  Issue No. 011  ·  Thursday, October 29, 2026

The hearing this paper tracked for a week produced its verdicts on Wednesday, and they split along the supply chain. At one end, SK Hynix — the South Korean company that makes the high-bandwidth memory inside AI accelerators — reported the most profitable quarter in its history: revenue of 104.6 trillion won, operating profit of 83.3 trillion won, a margin near 80%. At the other end, the two largest American buyers of that hardware reported the same quarter from the opposite side. Microsoft’s cash flow stayed positive. Alphabet’s went negative for a second straight quarter, and Alphabet has raised $147.2 billion from outside investors this year to keep the spending funded.

The suppliers’ position is not only today’s margin. SK Hynix said long-term agreements with 14 customers already cover more than half of its planned production for 2027 and 2028. Broadcom’s $63 billion vehicle for Anthropic exists because Broadcom guarantees the chips’ residual value (Edition 009). Nvidia, AMD and Broadcom committed $4 billion between them to Meta’s stock offering (Edition 009). In each case the seller of a scarce input has converted this year’s scarcity into contracted future revenue, a guarantee, or an equity position in the customer. Scarcity ends; contracts signed during scarcity do not end with it.

The buyers are not in one position either, and Wednesday separated them. Microsoft spent $45.2 billion in the quarter, below what analysts expected, and collected $18.6 billion of free cash flow; its shares rose. Alphabet spent $51.4 billion, above expectations, and its free cash flow was negative $9.1 billion; its shares fell about 10% after hours. Both companies grew. The market’s response tracked the funding, not the growth — which suggests the question investors are now asking of the buildout is narrower than whether AI demand is real. It is who ends up holding the cost if the returns arrive later than the spending.

Two dates carry the question forward. Amazon reports tonight, the last of the three hyperscalers on this week’s calendar, with its own spending guide to defend. On Friday, signatures are due on OpenAI’s bridge — the financed layer’s turn to show whether capital is still available at the terms on offer, in a week when the Fed held rates with two members voting to raise them. The suppliers have already answered for their side of the bill. The buyers and the borrowers answer next.
