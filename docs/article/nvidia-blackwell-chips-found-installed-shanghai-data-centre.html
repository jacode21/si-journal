Nvidia’s Most Powerful AI Chips Were Found Installed in a Shanghai Data Centre

Two Chinese companies have installed Nvidia’s most powerful Blackwell AI systems at a data centre in Shanghai, Bloomberg reported on Friday, November 27, from documents the companies submitted to Chinese regulators in November. One system, a GB300 NVL72 rack — 72 Blackwell Ultra chips and 36 Grace processors in a cabinet weighing about 3,300 pounds — is identified in the filings by its serial number. The company that listed it, Shanghai Shuoyao, offered the rack as collateral for a 96 million yuan (about $14 million) loan from Shanghai Innovation Bank. A second company, Shanghai Kezhan, described a GB200 server, which also runs on Blackwell chips, at the same facility, the Yangtze River Delta Project. Blackwell systems may not be shipped to China without a United States licence, and Washington has never authorised their export there. How the hardware reached Shanghai is not established.
Edited by Avery Lane · Frontier Editor  ·  Issue No. 041  ·  Saturday, November 28, 2026

The facility is linked to Alice Huang, the founder of Megaspeed, a Singapore-based cloud company that became one of Nvidia’s largest customers in Southeast Asia. Bloomberg traced the GB300 rack’s serial number through a public warranty database to Gigabyte Technology of Taiwan, which declined to say whom it originally sold the hardware to. Shanghai Shuoyao has said it is one of Megaspeed’s service providers, and Megaspeed used renderings of the Yangtze River Delta Project in a presentation to investors in 2024 that did not list China as the location. The project’s president is Kang Wei, reported to be Alice Huang’s husband. Bloomberg states it has no evidence that Megaspeed itself diverted hardware to the site, and Megaspeed denies wrongdoing. The Justice Department is investigating whether Megaspeed broke US export rules, and the company was placed on a US trade blacklist this month (reported November 24). Filings also show servers with Nvidia’s earlier Hopper chips at the project; those, too, require a licence for China. The site is designed to hold as many as 800,000 servers.

The same day, one of the governments caught in this paper’s lead story yesterday answered on the record. Singapore’s Deputy Prime Minister Gan Kim Yong, speaking at the Asia Future Summit on Friday, said his government had not confirmed reports that Nvidia halted server shipments to Singapore, and was engaging local businesses to assess the impact (Bloomberg). Singapore is reviewing its own processes to guard against illicit chip trade, he said, and is talking to Washington to preserve its businesses’ access to advanced chips. Nvidia did not respond to questions about the Shanghai story. Its shares opened higher on Friday, reversed after the Bloomberg report, and ended the shortened session lower, on a day the Nasdaq Composite rose about 1.8% (MarketWatch). Jensen Huang has previously dismissed the idea that Blackwell systems could be smuggled, on the ground that the racks weigh nearly two tons.

What is missing is the route. Nobody has established who sold the racks, through which countries they travelled, or when they arrived; the loan documents date from November, and one related loan, for the GB200, was taken out in late 2025. The dated tests now sit in Washington: Huang and Nvidia’s general counsel are called to House questioning on December 1 (reported), and the company’s delayed quarterly report is due by December 4. The question those proceedings will put is narrower than the politics around it: whether a control system built on licences and supplier compliance can account for hardware that is already installed, financed and running in the country it was meant to keep out.

The Bigger Picture

Yesterday this paper reported the paperwork half of the new export controls: shipments stopped to four countries while licences are sorted out. Friday brought the physical half. The most restricted American hardware is not at a border or in a warehouse; it is installed in Shanghai, and it was found not by an inspector but by a bank. To borrow against a rack, a company must describe it — model, serial number, location — and file the description where regulators and reporters can read it. The control that produced the week’s clearest fact was not an export rule. It was a loan application.
