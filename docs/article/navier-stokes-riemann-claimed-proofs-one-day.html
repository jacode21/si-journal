Two Claimed Proofs in One Day: Navier-Stokes Without Force, and the Riemann Hypothesis, Both Built With AI Help and Neither Yet Verified

On Friday, November 20, Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge posted a note claiming that he and Claude had found a proof of the Riemann hypothesis, the most famous open problem in mathematics, formalised in Lean and extending, he said, to the generalised hypothesis. Hours earlier, Alpöge and New York University professor Tristan Buckmaster posted a 79-page paper claiming that the three-dimensional Navier-Stokes equations, with no external force applied, can blow up in finite time — the version of that problem mathematicians regard as the real one. Both claims are serious. Neither is settled: the Navier-Stokes paper’s central numerical certificate was not released, only a hash promising it, and the Riemann claim has no readable paper, only a Lean formalisation and a four-page note.
Edited by Avery Lane · Frontier Editor  ·  Issue No. 034  ·  Saturday, November 21, 2026

Start with what was actually posted. The Navier-Stokes paper, by Buckmaster and Alpöge, runs to 79 pages and claims smooth starting conditions under which the unforced three-dimensional equations blow up — a quantity becomes infinite in finite time (paper, Nov 20; Scientific American). It carries a Lean 4 formalisation of about 600,000 lines across roughly 2,270 files, built with heavy help from Anthropic and OpenAI models, including Codex; the authors state that reliance openly. The Riemann claim is thinner as a document: a four-page note on Alpöge’s website and a Lean formalisation split across 14 compressed files. In the note, Alpöge says Claude suggested the decisive step, and that the informal draft of the argument runs past 250,000 pages and that he had not mastered every part of it before announcing (note, Nov 20; The Condia; Gizmodo).

The gaps are specific, and they are the story’s centre. The Navier-Stokes result depends on a numerical certificate — the computed starting profile the analysis rests on — and that certificate is not in the repository; the paper gives a cryptographic hash and says it will be released soon, and the rigorous interval computation that would check the certificate is not part of the Lean formalisation. The posted Lean package was patched on November 20 and not rebuilt after the patch. For the Riemann claim, there is no human-readable proof to examine at all yet. Terence Tao’s early assessment, reported in the November 21 frontier briefing, is that verification of the Riemann artifact has only just started. The Clay Mathematics Institute, which offers $1 million for each problem under its own rules, has confirmed neither claim.

The context is a race, and the race explains the rush. OpenAI announced a Navier-Stokes blowup result in September, using a finely tuned external force that many experts regarded as a loophole in the problem’s wording; Buckmaster publicly alleged OpenAI had scooped his and Alpöge’s work, which OpenAI denied (Scientific American). Buckmaster wrote that the new paper was posted unpolished because, after last time, the pair could not risk waiting. A proof posted to win a race and a proof that has survived checking are different objects; Friday produced two of the first kind. The checking is now the news to watch.
