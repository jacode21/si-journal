SoftBank Paid for Data Centre Equipment in Advance and Has Written Off $3.7 Billion of It

SoftBank has written off $3.7 billion of equipment for data centres that it paid for in advance and that was never delivered, Bloomberg reported, inside a July-to-September quarter that produced a net loss of 1.12 trillion yen — about $7.1 billion — against a consensus forecast of a profit, and the group’s first loss in almost two years. The payments were non-refundable. The Information put the total committed to such prepayments at $9.5 billion, so the write-off covers part of the exposure, not all of it.
Edited by Avery Lane · Frontier Editor  ·  Issue No. 024  ·  Wednesday, November 11, 2026

Every shortage economy teaches the same move: if the equipment is scarce, pay early. In the AI buildout, prepayment became standard practice — money sent to suppliers months or years before delivery, to hold a place in the queue for chips, servers and power gear. SoftBank’s quarter shows what that practice is, in accounting terms. A prepayment is not inventory. It is an unsecured loan to the supplier, made by the buyer, at zero interest, repayable in equipment. While the plan holds, the loan is invisible. When the plan changes, the loan is repriced in public.

The facts, as reported: SoftBank wrote off $3.7 billion in the July-to-September quarter on data centre equipment paid for in advance. The wider quarter lost 1.12 trillion yen net, about $7.1 billion, where analysts expected a profit — the group’s first quarterly loss in almost two years. Bloomberg reported the write-off; The Information reported the committed prepayment total at $9.5 billion against equipment never delivered, with the payments non-refundable. On those figures, $3.7 billion is the portion now written down; the remaining exposure depends on deliveries and terms that have not been published.

Place it in the buildout’s ledger, which this paper has kept since October. The capex thread began with buyers racing to spend; it moved to suppliers financing their own customers, in Edition 008’s chain of landlords; then to credit markets pricing the borrowers apart, in Edition 016’s two-tier money. SoftBank’s entry is the buyer-side version of the same lesson: the risk in the buildout no longer sits only in whether the demand arrives. It sits in the chain of promises made before anything is delivered — prepayments, capacity reservations, take-or-pay contracts — each one reasonable while supply is short, each one a loss the moment the plan moves. Anthropic’s near-$900 billion of commitments, CoreWeave’s $46.9 billion of debt and SoftBank’s $3.7 billion write-off are three denominations of one instrument: money committed ahead of delivery, at prices set in a shortage.

What would change the assessment: evidence that the remaining prepayments convert into delivered, working equipment on the original schedule. Until then, the number to watch in every buildout filing is not the headline capex. It is the prepayment line — how much cash has already left, against goods that have not yet arrived, on terms the buyer cannot reverse.
