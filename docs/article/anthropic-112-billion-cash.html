Anthropic Holds $112 Billion in Cash, and Its Customers Can Cancel at Short Notice

The market’s first verdict on the filed prospectus arrived Monday — and so did the details the leaked draft never carried: $112.4 billion in cash, a second quarter in operating profit, a benefit trust with a former Federal Reserve chair among its trustees, and a revenue base the filing itself describes as cancellable at short notice.
Edited by Avery Lane · Frontier Editor  ·  Issue No. 008  ·  Tuesday, October 27, 2026

Day two of the public Anthropic. Yesterday’s Lead covered the filing itself; this story covers what a full read of the document — and the market’s first full session with it — adds.

The tape’s verdict

Monday’s close: the S&P 500 rose 0.70% to 7,676.77, the Nasdaq Composite 0.83% to 26,169.38 and the Dow 0.28% to 52,361.58 — a recovery from Friday’s losses that market coverage attributed first to the filing. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index rose 2.82% — Nvidia +2.39%, Micron +2.98%, AMD +2.80%, Broadcom +2.33% — and the move ran through Asia, where SK Hynix and Samsung each gained more than 4%. Two qualifications belong in the same paragraph: the SOX remains down 12.2% for October, and the day was not a blanket AI rally — Alphabet fell about 1% ahead of its Wednesday report, and Meta fell 1.88% on its own financing news (see Latest). Oil helped the tape rather than the thesis: Brent eased 0.78% to $115.81 as Saudi and Iranian officials talked, and the 10-year Treasury held at 5.00%.

The cash

The filing’s balance sheet is the first surprise. At June 30, Anthropic held $112.4 billion in cash and a further $19.3 billion in short-term investments — before the offering adds a dollar. It has raised more than $140 billion since 2021, on a ladder the coverage of the document makes plain: about $13 billion at a $183 billion valuation a year ago, $30 billion at $380 billion in February, $65 billion at $965 billion in May. It has also expanded its revolving credit facility from $3 billion to $15 billion. Whatever the roadshow concludes about price, the company negotiating it is not short of money.

The operating picture

The second surprise is how close to break-even the business underneath the paper loss already is. The first-half GAAP operating loss was $693 million — about 4% of the $16.3 billion in revenue — and the second quarter produced roughly $1 billion in operating income. On the adjusted basis the company presents to investors, the operating margin was 10% in the first half and 11% in the third quarter. The engine is compute efficiency: compute consumed 55% of revenue in the first half, against 201% a year earlier, and the founders’ letter puts the cost at under 50 cents of compute per revenue dollar in the June quarter, against more than $2.50 in the first quarter of 2025. The same letter concedes the commitments “may seem irresponsibly large at first glance” and calls them “bold, but sane.”

The mismatch

Fortune’s read of the document names the tension the roadshow will be priced on: much of Anthropic’s revenue lacks long-term commitments — contracts allow customers to reduce usage or terminate with limited or no notice — while the spending is fixed years ahead. Counted as binding obligations, Fortune puts the compute and infrastructure commitments at $542–567 billion; the higher totals in circulation include options and maximums, among them up to $150 billion more still under negotiation. The prospectus also reveals that the company plans to build its own data centres, rather than only renting everyone else’s. Its largest single commitment, meanwhile, is a $161 billion, five-year lease of Google TPUs through Broadcom (see The Long View).

The governance, in the document

The syndicate runs to 22 underwriters, Goldman Sachs lead-left and Morgan Stanley as stabilization agent. The control structure the draft promised is in the filed text: the seven co-founders’ Class F share — no economic interest, 50.1% of the vote — and a Long-Term Benefit Trust that appoints a board majority and cannot remove Dario or Daniela Amodei. Fortune’s reading adds the texture: the trust’s seated trustees include Ben Bernanke, the former Federal Reserve chair; the founders’ special share lapses if fewer than three of the seven remain in the group; and each co-founder has pledged to give away 80% of their shares.

Why it matters

Yesterday’s Position argued the frontier was being priced off a leak. The leak is now a document, and the document is better and worse than the leak in specific, arguable ways: the cash is larger, the operations closer to profit, the customer base less contractually bound than the spending it must pay for. Reported valuation talk sits near $1.5 trillion (Reuters), against a reported aim of up to $2 trillion and a raise of up to $100 billion; the roadshow in the week of November 9 is where the argument happens. Before that, Wednesday’s hearing — the Fed, Microsoft, Alphabet — prices the sector this filing now anchors.
