Three Labs Reported Proofs a Computer Had Checked Line by Line in One Week, and the Checking Is the Story

Between November 5 and 8, models from three labs — OpenAI, Anthropic-linked work via Physical Superintelligence, and Moonshot AI — produced results in Lean, a proof assistant that accepts or rejects each step mechanically, while Google DeepMind and a Meta employee offered a promise and a teaser. On the TCSExam benchmark paper posted to arXiv on November 5, GPT-6 Astra produced a complete, machine-checked proof in 70.0% of attempts across 20 research theorems, and resolved none of the benchmark’s 30 open conjectures. Counts and classifications are FourWeekMBA’s, from the underlying reports.
Edited by Avery Lane · Frontier Editor  ·  Issue No. 022  ·  Monday, November 9, 2026

There is a hierarchy of evidence in AI science, and this week displayed every level of it at once. At the top sit results checked by a machine. Lean is a proof assistant: software that accepts a mathematical proof only if every step follows, with no room for a confident tone to carry a weak argument. Between November 5 and 8, three labs reported Lean-checked results. OpenAI’s GPT-6 Astra, on the TCSExam benchmark posted to arXiv on November 5, produced complete machine-checked proofs in 70.0% of attempts across 20 research theorems — and solved none of the same benchmark’s 30 open conjectures, the problems nobody has solved. Moonshot’s Kimi K3.1 Pro came second on the theorem track at 38.3%. Physical Superintelligence, a research lab, said an internal system running a non-public Anthropic model found a proof of the Aaronson–Ambainis conjecture, a problem open since 2008, and that Lean accepted it — the lab’s announcement; which Anthropic model was used is not clear in the reporting.

Below that level sit scores on tests a lab or a benchmark author designed, where the grading is real but the questions were chosen by someone with an interest in the answer. Below those sit announcements of capability with the evidence held back — the category of Anthropic’s own research-use study this week, solid on the sessions it counted and modeled, not measured, on the acceleration it projected. And at the bottom, this week, sit two communications with no evidence at all: Pushmeet Kohli of Google DeepMind posted on November 8 that readers “will see a glimpse of this in our releases in the next few weeks,” and a Meta employee answered, “Wait till you see what we been up at @Meta.” Neither names a result, a date or a test.

The pattern is worth naming because it inverts the usual coverage. The machine-checked results are, on their face, the week’s most modest claims — theorems proved at a stated rate, conjectures untouched. They are also the only claims in the set that a skeptic can settle without trusting anyone: download the proof, run the checker. Sunday’s Long View in this paper (Edition 021) showed agents failing an integrity check on work nobody re-ran. The same week contained the opposite discipline, in the same field. The difference between AI science that accumulates and AI science that circulates is, increasingly, nothing more exotic than whether the work arrives with its checking attached.
