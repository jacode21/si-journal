The Power Behind a Model Is Part of What the Model Costs

A case study of Anthropic's contracted data centres, published November 16 by the Better Data Center Project, a nonprofit founded by two former US Department of Energy officials, estimates that about 87% of the power behind the company's US computing in 2028 will come from gas and about 11% from coal. It estimates emissions of 17 to 38 million tonnes of carbon dioxide a year, with a middle case of 32 million — more, it notes, than all of California's power plants produced in 2024. These are the project's estimates, built from the sites Anthropic has signed for and models of which power plants would serve them. They are not Anthropic's figures, and Anthropic has not published a 2028 power mix.
Edited by Avery Lane · Frontier Editor  ·  Issue No. 036  ·  Monday, November 23, 2026

The study's method is site by site. Anthropic had contracted about 15 gigawatts of computing power by October — a gigawatt is roughly the output of a large power station — with 5.9 to 9.6 gigawatts tied to specific sites. About a quarter of that power, roughly 2.5 gigawatts, is to come from gas plants built next to the data centres themselves, a practice the industry calls bring-your-own-power; the study says 80% of that on-site capacity uses simpler, less efficient turbines and engines. Separately, it reports that Anthropic has bought commitments for 1.7 gigawatts of heavy-duty gas generators for $3.4 billion. It also finds no direct commitments by Anthropic to renewable energy in the United States, with its disclosed renewable contracts in Norway and Canada.

Read carefully, the study does not show a company choosing gas over clean power in a market where both were available on the same terms. It shows what happens when computing is contracted faster than clean power can be built and connected: the power that can arrive on the data centre's schedule is gas, on site or from the nearest grid, and the grid in the places the centres are sited runs mainly on gas and coal. That is the same constraint this paper has tracked all autumn in other forms — permits in Mississippi, grid queues in London, turbines at SpaceX's Colossus site, where the study notes Anthropic has used capacity since May and may draw up to 600 megawatts by the end of 2026.

What changes, if the estimate is broadly right, is where the argument about AI's cost has to be held. The filing Anthropic published last week counts its commitments in dollars: about $900 billion across the life of the contracts (Edition 035). The power study counts the same buildout in fuel. A reader who wants to judge the buildout needs both ledgers, and at the moment only one of them is the company's own. Anthropic's approach document from October says it follows the air-permitting process everywhere it operates and requires the same of the plants powering its centres. The test the study sets, without stating it, is whether a US renewable commitment appears before the 2028 capacity does.
