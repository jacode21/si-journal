Yesterday this paper reported that SpaceX has permission to build data centres in orbit, with the first satellite not required until 2033. A Chinese company already has a small one up there, and it has now been tested by an outside body. Adaspace’s Libra satellite took a full-size AI model — Alibaba’s Qwen3-32B — and trained it continuously for 45 minutes and 33 seconds during a single pass around the Earth: the model attempted problems, checked its wrong answers, and adjusted itself, all on a computer bolted inside a satellite. The test also found the satellite’s cooling system — the problem sceptics raised about SpaceX’s plan — kept a 10,000-watt computer within its limits for the run.

What it is

Adaspace, a space-AI company listed in Hong Kong on November 13, launched the second group of its Star-Compute constellation — eight satellites — from Jiuquan on October 14, 2026. The China Academy of Information and Communications Technology, a research body under China’s industry ministry, then ran the first third-party tests in orbit and published the results this week. One satellite, Libra, carries computing power the academy measured at 32 PFLOPS — roughly 32 quadrillion simple calculations a second, in the compact number format AI uses. Across the constellation, total computing power is about 50 POPS. The academy verified training and inference (answering questions) for the Qwen3-32B model, and the 45-minute-33-second continuous training run is, on the published record, the longest of its kind in a single orbit — about three times a roughly 15-minute run reported for a Google prototype.

Why it matters

Training is the heavy half of AI — the part that fills data centres and waits for grid connections. Doing any of it in orbit, even for three-quarters of an hour, moves orbital computing from a slide to a measurement, and it answers the first of the objections filed against SpaceX’s million-satellite application: heat. Libra shed the heat of a 10-kilowatt payload — about ten household space heaters’ worth — using a liquid cooling loop and large radiating wings. That is a small machine next to a ground data centre, and one run is not a service. But the order of events has flipped: the working demonstration now belongs to the smaller, earlier system, and the million-satellite permission belongs to the company that has yet to fly one.

What is not yet known

The tester is a Chinese state research academy and the announcement is the company’s; no Western or fully independent body has replicated the measurements. “World record” claims rest on publicly available information, as the company itself phrases it. A 45-minute training run on one model shows a capability, not an economics: nothing published says what the computing cost, against the same work done on the ground.

Evidence — Emerging technology

Adaspace’s announcement of November 26–27, 2026, reporting the China Academy of Information and Communications Technology’s first batch of third-party in-orbit test results: 32 PFLOPS nominal for Libra; Qwen3-32B training and inference verified; 45 minutes 33 seconds of continuous full-parameter training in one orbit; the 10 kW-class liquid-cooling system and radiating wings operating normally. The constellation plan totals 2,800 satellites; two groups are in orbit.
